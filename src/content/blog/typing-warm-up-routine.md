The first two minutes of a typing session are almost always the worst two minutes. Error rates run higher, rhythm is uneven, and speed sits well below where it will settle. If you take a test cold, you are measuring your worst performance and calling it your baseline.

A five-minute warm-up fixes most of that. It is worth understanding why, because the common explanation is wrong.

## Warming up is not about warm muscles

The phrase comes from athletics, where raising muscle temperature genuinely improves contraction speed and reduces injury risk. Typing does not work this way. The forearm muscles doing the work are small, the load is trivial, and nothing about a five-minute drill meaningfully changes tissue temperature.

What actually improves is motor readiness — the state of the neural systems that plan and execute the movement.

Several things are going on. **Motor pattern retrieval** is the main one: the sequences mapping letters to finger movements are stored, but the first executions after a break are slower and more variable than later ones, as the pattern is reloaded and stabilised. **Sensorimotor calibration** matters too — your fingers need a few dozen keystrokes to adapt to this keyboard's key travel, actuation force and spacing, which is why the first minute on an unfamiliar keyboard feels clumsy even for experienced typists. There is also an **attentional** component: shifting into focused visual tracking and prediction takes a little time.

None of this is exotic. It is the same reason a musician plays scales before a performance, and it is worth roughly 5 to 15 WPM compared with starting cold.

## The routine

Five minutes, five stages. Keep the whole thing at a pace that feels comfortable rather than pushed.

### Minute 0: hands and posture (30 seconds)

Before touching the keyboard, do three things.

Shake your hands loosely at the wrists for five seconds. Then, with your arm extended and palm down, gently pull the fingers of that hand back with the other hand until you feel a mild stretch in the forearm, hold ten seconds, and repeat with the palm facing up. Do both arms. Keep it gentle — aggressive stretching before fine motor work does not help and can slightly reduce precision.

Then set your posture deliberately: feet flat, elbows at roughly 90 to 110 degrees, wrists neutral rather than bent upward, shoulders down. This takes five seconds and it is the highest-value part of the routine, because posture set at the start tends to persist through the session.

### Minutes 0:30 to 1:30: home row runs

Type these slowly and evenly, with your eyes off the keyboard:

```
asdf jkl; asdf jkl;
fdsa ;lkj fdsa ;lkj
asdfjkl; ;lkjfdsa
```

Then the same with the index fingers reaching:

```
fgfg jhjh fgfg jhjh
frf juj fvf jmj
```

The purpose is anchoring. You are re-establishing the home position and confirming that your fingers find F and J by feel. Rhythm matters more than speed here — aim for an even beat, like a metronome, rather than bursts.

### Minutes 1:30 to 3:00: bigrams and trigrams

Most typing speed lives in the letter pairs and triples that recur constantly in English. These are the units your hands should execute as single movements rather than as separate keystrokes.

The highest-frequency English bigrams are `th`, `he`, `in`, `er`, `an`, `re`, `on`, `at`, `en`, `nd`. The common trigrams are `the`, `and`, `ing`, `ion`, `ent`, `tio`, `for`, `her`.

Drill them in short blocks:

```
th th th the the the
in in ing ing ing
er er her her ther
and and and nd nd
tion tion ation ation
```

Then the awkward ones, which vary by person but commonly include:

```
ght ght right night
que que quick quiet
ph ph graph phone
```

Ninety seconds of this does more for practical speed than the same time on random text, because you are drilling exactly the sequences that recur every few words.

### Minutes 3:00 to 4:00: speed ramp

Type ordinary sentences and deliberately step the pace up. Twenty seconds at roughly 70% of your comfortable speed — slow, controlled, zero errors. Twenty seconds at normal speed. Twenty seconds slightly above it, fast enough to feel the edge of control but not so fast that accuracy collapses.

The ramp lets you locate your current ceiling rather than assuming it. On a good day the top step feels easy; on a tired day it falls apart, which is useful information before you commit to a long session. Come back down to normal pace for the last few seconds — do not start real work at the top of the ramp.

### Minutes 4:00 to 5:00: the calibration test

Finish with a one-minute test, treated as a measurement rather than a performance.

The distinction matters. A test taken as a score-chase changes how you type: you push past your controlled speed, tense up, and the number describes your maximum effort under pressure rather than your working state. A calibration test is taken at the pace you actually intend to work at, with accuracy as the primary reading.

What you want is the relationship between the two numbers. Accuracy at or above 97% with speed near your usual figure means you are ready. Accuracy holding but speed 10% low usually means fatigue — plan a shorter session. Speed normal but accuracy at 93% means you are typing above your controlled rate today, and the fix is to slow down. Both numbers well below normal is a signal to check posture and sleep before blaming technique.

One minute is deliberately short: long enough to be informative, short enough that a bad run costs you nothing.

## When to skip it

A warm-up is worth most before a long session, before any test you care about, first thing in the morning, and when moving to an unfamiliar keyboard. It is worth much less if you have been typing for the last ten minutes already — the pattern is loaded, and there is nothing to reload.

It is also not a substitute for practice. A warm-up recovers performance you already have; it does not build new capability. Someone who warms up religiously and never practises deliberately keeps hitting the same ceiling, just from a slightly better starting position.

To put the routine into practice, a [1-minute typing test](/typing-test-1-minute/) works well as the calibration step at the end, and structured [typing lessons](/typing-lessons/) are the right place to drill the specific bigrams that keep tripping you up. If you want the warm-up to become automatic, attaching it to a [daily challenge](/daily-challenge/) gives it a consistent slot rather than leaving it to whether you remember.

Most people who want to type faster do the same thing: they take typing tests. They take one, see a number, take another, and hope the number goes up. It rarely does, or it creeps up so slowly that they give up.

The problem is that testing is measurement, not practice. Measuring your weight every day does not make you lighter. What follows is what actually moves the number.

## Accuracy comes before speed, always

This is the single most important thing to understand, and it is the opposite of what most people do.

When you type faster than your accuracy allows, you are not practising fast typing. You are practising making errors. Every mistake you make trains your hands to make that mistake again, and the correction — noticing, backspacing, retyping — costs far more time than typing slowly would have.

The rule: **keep your accuracy above 97%.** If you drop below that, slow down until you are back above it. Your speed will feel worse for a week and then overtake where you started.

A typist at 70 WPM with 99% accuracy finishes real work faster than one at 90 WPM with 90% accuracy. The second typist makes an error every ten characters, and each one interrupts the flow of thought as well as the flow of keystrokes.

## Practise slightly above comfortable

Motor skills develop at the edge of your current ability. Typing at a speed you have already mastered maintains that speed; it does not extend it.

The useful zone is roughly 10–20% above your comfortable pace — fast enough that you feel slight strain and have to concentrate, slow enough that accuracy holds above 97%. Too far past that and you collapse into error-practice. Too far below and nothing changes.

This means your practice sessions should feel mildly uncomfortable. If practice feels easy, it is not practice.

## Find your actual weak points

Typists usually have a handful of specific problems, not a general slowness. Common culprits:

- **Number row.** Most courses barely teach it, so most typists look down for digits.
- **Symbols.** Brackets, braces and operators sit at the keyboard edges and use the weakest fingers.
- **Specific letter transitions.** Awkward same-hand sequences that break rhythm.
- **Capitals.** Reaching for shift with the wrong hand, or lifting a hand off the home row entirely.

Fixing one genuine weak point beats a hundred general tests. If numbers are your bottleneck, general practice will barely touch them, because a normal text prompt contains almost no digits.

## Short sessions, frequently

Muscle memory responds to frequency far more than duration. Ten to fifteen minutes a day beats a two-hour session once a week, comfortably.

There is also a practical reason: after about twenty minutes, fatigue sets in, accuracy drops, and you start practising errors again. The long session actively undoes some of its own work.

## Do not look at your hands

If you look down, you are not touch typing — you are doing a fast version of hunt-and-peck, and it has a hard ceiling somewhere around 40–50 WPM.

Looking down feels faster in the moment because you avoid errors. It is slower over any timescale that matters, because your eyes have to leave the screen and find their place again on every glance.

If you cannot type without looking, that is the thing to fix first, before speed enters the picture at all. It will feel dramatically worse for a few days. Push through it.

## What a realistic timeline looks like

- **Weeks 1–2:** If you are correcting technique, expect to get *slower*. This is normal and temporary.
- **Weeks 3–6:** You return to your old speed with better form and much better accuracy.
- **Months 2–4:** Steady gains, typically 10–20 WPM above where you started.
- **Beyond:** Progress slows and becomes about specific weaknesses rather than general speed.

Anyone promising 100 WPM in a week is selling something.

Almost nobody fails to improve their typing because the practice methods were wrong. They fail because they practised for nine days and then stopped. The technique questions — which drills, which order — matter far less than whether you are still doing it in six weeks.

## Frequency beats duration, and there is a reason

Fifteen minutes a day, six days a week, is roughly 90 minutes. Two hours on a Sunday is more total time. The daily version produces substantially better results, and the reason is not willpower.

Motor skills consolidate *between* practice sessions rather than during them. Performance improves within a session, but the memory trace laid down is initially unstable. Over the following hours — particularly during sleep — the nervous system reprocesses it, and the skill is measurably better at the start of the next session than at the end of the last. This offline gain is well documented in finger-sequence tasks. So consolidation opportunities are counted in sessions, not minutes: six sessions a week give six cycles, one long session gives one.

A second effect works the same direction. Within a session, improvement flattens after roughly 20 to 30 minutes, and fatigue then starts reinforcing sloppy patterns. The last 90 minutes of a two-hour session are close to wasted. Ten to fifteen minutes is the sweet spot: long enough for real work, short enough that you will do it on a bad day.

## Attach it to something that already happens

The most reliable way to make a new behaviour stick is to anchor it to an existing one. Habit stacking means specifying *after [existing routine], I will [new behaviour]* rather than resolving to do it "daily." The failure mode of a new habit is rarely unwillingness — it is forgetting, never having a moment where the intention gets triggered.

Good anchors are specific, daily and already automatic: after I open my laptop and before opening email; after my first coffee is poured. Poor anchors are vague ("in the morning"), unreliable ("when I have a spare moment"), or attached to something you skip regularly.

Physical setup matters too: if the practice site is not already open in a pinned tab, you have added a decision point, and decision points are where habits die.

## Streaks: useful, and quietly harmful

Streak counters work. Loss aversion is a real and strong effect — people will exert more effort to avoid losing a 40-day streak than to gain something of equivalent value. For the first few weeks, a visible counter is among the more effective tools available. The problems appear later.

**Streak anxiety.** Once a streak feels valuable, the daily session becomes something you must do. People practise at 11:50 pm, exhausted, purely to protect a number, and fatigued practice reinforces poor patterns.

**All-or-nothing collapse.** The destructive failure mode. You miss a day, the streak resets, and the structure motivating you evaporates. Many people who abandon a habit do so within days of breaking a long streak — not because one missed day mattered, but because the counter was the only thing holding the habit up.

**Quantity over quality.** A streak rewards showing up, not practising well.

Two adjustments help. Build tolerance in — aim for five sessions a week rather than seven, so a missed day is within the plan. And keep a secondary count that never resets, so a broken streak sets back one number instead of erasing everything. Missing one day is noise; missing two in a row is where the pattern breaks.

## Goals that survive contact with reality

"Reach 100 WPM" is a poor goal for someone at 45 — not because it is unachievable, but because it is twelve months away and gives no feedback in the meantime, which means twelve months of feeling like a failure.

Better goals are close enough to reach in weeks, specific enough that you know whether you hit them, and at least partly about process rather than outcome, because process is under your control and results are not. Examples: hold 97% accuracy across five consecutive tests; complete twenty sessions this month; stop the semicolon and Q reaches generating errors.

The honest figures: a beginner going from 25 to 45 WPM can expect that in six to eight weeks of daily practice. Someone at 60 aiming for 75 should plan on three to four months. Above 80, progress is measured in a few WPM per quarter and requires deliberate work on specific weaknesses, because general practice stops producing gains.

## Read the trend, not the score

A single test result is mostly noise. Sleep, caffeine, time of day, keyboard, the particular text — these move an individual result by 10 to 15 WPM in either direction for the same typist on the same day. That produces a demoralising pattern: you test well one day, worse the next, and conclude you are getting worse. You are watching variance.

The fix is to look at a rolling average across five or ten recent sessions, and at the shape of the line over weeks rather than today's position. A trend that has moved from 52 to 56 over a month is real progress even if today's test came in at 49.

Log something beyond speed. Accuracy is usually the more informative number, and a rising accuracy figure at flat speed is genuine improvement a WPM-only view misses. Consistency tightens before the average moves, so a narrowing spread is an early sign something is working.

Expect plateaus. Motor learning goes in steps, with flat stretches where consolidation happens without visible gain. A three-week plateau is normal. A three-month plateau is a reason to look at what is limiting you — usually a small set of keys or a technique fault, not a lack of practice.

## Getting started

Pick a time, attach it to something you already do, and keep the first sessions short enough to repeat easily. A [daily challenge](/daily-challenge/) gives the habit a fixed shape and removes the daily question of what to practise. Reviewing your [statistics](/statistics/) once a week — not once a day — keeps you looking at trends rather than reacting to single results. When the trend flattens, structured [typing lessons](/typing-lessons/) aimed at your weak keys will do more than another month of general practice.

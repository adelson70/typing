Touch typing means typing without looking at the keyboard. That is the entire definition. It is not about speed — speed is a consequence — but about your fingers knowing where keys are, so your attention stays on what you are writing.

Most self-taught typists reach 30 to 50 WPM with hunt-and-peck, then stop improving for the rest of their lives. The ceiling is not effort: visual search cannot be made much faster, and every glance down costs you your place in the text.

## The home row

Rest your left hand on **A S D F** and your right on **J K L ;**, thumbs on the space bar. That is the home row, the position your hands return to after every reach.

Run a fingertip over F and J. Both have a small raised bump. These exist so you can find the home row by touch alone — put your index fingers on the bumps, let the others fall into place, and your hands are positioned. Learn to trust this. It is the mechanism that makes the method work, and it means you can resume typing after any interruption without a visual check.

## Which finger presses which key

Each finger owns a roughly vertical column, reaching up or down from its home key and then returning.

On the left hand, the little finger takes Q A Z plus Shift, Tab and Caps Lock; the ring finger W S X; the middle finger E D C; and the index finger covers two columns, R F V and T G B. On the right, the index finger again covers two columns, U J M and Y H N; the middle finger takes I K and the comma, the ring finger O L and the full stop, and the little finger P, semicolon and apostrophe plus Shift, Enter and the brackets.

Both index fingers do double duty, which is why they handle the most frequent letters. The little fingers are weakest, and they get the rarest letters plus the modifiers.

Shift is the most commonly broken rule. Use the **opposite** hand's Shift from the letter you are capitalising: capital A uses the right Shift, capital P the left. Same-hand shifting forces the little finger into an awkward stretch.

## The rule that matters most

Do not look at your hands. Not once. Not "just to check this key."

This is the hardest part and the one that determines whether you actually learn. Every time you look down, you reinforce the visual lookup pathway instead of muscle memory, and the two compete. Learners who allow themselves occasional glances typically take two or three times longer to become fluent. If you cannot resist, remove the option: a keyboard cover, a tea towel, or blank keycaps all work.

When you cannot remember where a key is, pause, think about which finger owns it and which direction it reaches, then try. Getting it wrong and correcting is more useful than looking it up, because the retrieval attempt builds the memory.

## The part nobody warns you about

You will get slower. Substantially slower, and it will last longer than you expect.

If you currently hunt-and-peck at 40 WPM, your first week of touch typing will run at 10 to 15 WPM. Typing one sentence will take real effort. Many people quit here, conclude it "isn't for them," and go back to what worked.

This regression is not failure. Your existing method is an automated pattern built over years; the new one is a deliberate sequence rebuilt from scratch, and during the overlap the old pattern interferes with the new. That is why partial commitment is worse than full commitment. The regression is temporary. The plateau you were on was not.

## A realistic timeline

These figures assume 15 to 30 minutes of daily practice. Less frequent practice stretches everything out.

**Week 1.** Home row first, then the most common top and bottom row letters. Roughly 10 WPM at the start, 15 to 20 by the end, and every keystroke deliberate.

**Weeks 2 to 3.** All letters covered. Basic competence: you can type a sentence without looking, slowly. Typically 20 to 30 WPM, and common letter pairs start to feel automatic.

**Weeks 4 to 6.** Punctuation, capitals, numbers. Speed climbs to 30 to 40 WPM, and most learners cross back over their old hunt-and-peck rate.

**Months 2 to 3.** Comfortably past your old speed, typically 40 to 55 WPM, and — more importantly — typing without thinking about typing. This is where the skill pays for itself, because your attention is free for the writing. Beyond that, 60 to 80 WPM over six months to a year.

If you are slower than these numbers, that is normal variation. The sequence matters more than the schedule.

## How to practise

**Short and daily.** Fifteen minutes a day beats two hours on Sunday. Motor learning consolidates between sessions, particularly during sleep, so frequency does work that duration cannot.

**Accuracy before speed, always.** Aim for 97% or better and let speed arrive on its own. Push speed while accuracy is poor and you are practising your errors, which become automatic too.

**Drill your weak keys.** After a fortnight you will notice the same three or four keys causing most errors — often little-finger reaches like Q, P, Z and the semicolon. General practice barely touches them because they are rare in English; targeted repetition fixes them in days.

**Type real text, not just drills.** Letter exercises build key positions; sentences build rhythm and the ability to read ahead. Weight it toward real text after the first fortnight.

**Watch for tension.** Shoulders creeping up, jaw clenching, fingers pressing too hard — all easy to build in during the frustrating early weeks.

## Where to start

Begin with structured [typing lessons](/typing-lessons/) that introduce keys in a deliberate order rather than throwing the whole keyboard at you on day one. Once the letters are covered, a [1-minute typing test](/typing-test-1-minute/) is a useful low-pressure check — short enough that a bad run costs nothing, frequent enough to show a trend. Adding a [daily challenge](/daily-challenge/) once you are past the worst of the regression gives the practice a shape, which matters when motivation dips in week two.
